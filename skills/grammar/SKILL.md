# So, thus, therefore, hence

All four words signal a consequence, but they are not interchangeable. The differences are grammatical (what the word can connect), punctuational, and stylistic (register and meaning).

## The core rule: one conjunction, three adverbs

"So" is a coordinating conjunction. It can join two independent clauses with just a comma:

- ✅ The printer was jammed, so I emailed the file instead.

"Thus", "therefore" and "hence" are adverbs (roughly "consequently"). An adverb cannot join two independent clauses on its own, so a comma before it creates a comma splice:

- ❌ The data was incomplete, therefore we postponed the analysis.
- ❌ The road was flooded, thus the bus took a detour.
- ❌ Demand fell sharply, hence we reduced production.

To fix the splice, do one of the following:

1. Start a new sentence: "The data was incomplete. Therefore, we postponed the analysis."
2. Use a semicolon: "The data was incomplete; therefore, we postponed the analysis."
3. Add "and": "The data was incomplete, and therefore we postponed the analysis."
4. Switch to "so", especially in less formal text: "The data was incomplete, so we postponed the analysis."

A clause is independent if it has its own subject and finite verb ("we postponed", "the bus took"). The rule only applies when the adverb introduces such a clause; see the special constructions below for cases where a comma before "thus" or "hence" is correct.

Some writers treat "therefore" as a conjunction and defend the comma. Major dictionaries do not record that usage, so in edited or formal text treat it as an error. In very casual text, mention it lightly rather than insisting.

## Punctuation around the adverbs

Style guides usually set "thus" and "therefore" off with commas ("Therefore, we..."; "; thus, the..."), but writers often drop the commas when they would interrupt the flow or pile up. For example, "and, thus, the" is usually written "and thus the". Both versions are acceptable, so don't "correct" a missing comma after one of these words unless the user's style guide requires it.

Commas around "hence" are dropped even more often than around "thus", especially in technical writing.

## Special constructions where a comma is correct

**"Thus" + -ing phrase.** "Thus" can introduce a participial phrase meaning "in doing so / as a result of this". No new clause follows, so the comma is correct:

- ✅ The city added bike lanes on the main avenue, thus reducing car traffic downtown.

**"Hence" + noun phrase.** This is the most common modern use of "hence". It replaces a verb like "which explains" or "which is the reason for", and it is followed by a noun phrase, not a clause. It is always preceded by a comma:

- ✅ The flight left two hours late, hence the missed connection.
- ✅ The plant closes its leaves when touched, hence its nickname "sensitive plant".

When editing, check what follows the word: a noun phrase or an -ing phrase means the comma can stay; a subject plus finite verb means it's a splice.

## Meaning and register

- **so**: neutral and universal. It is the default in speech and informal writing; the other three almost never appear in casual conversation.
- **thus**: formal. Expresses consequence or "in this way".
- **therefore**: formal. Strongest sense of logical deduction, so it is common in arguments, proofs and academic writing ("The function is continuous on a closed interval; therefore it attains a maximum."). It sounds odd when the link is merely practical or narrative rather than logical:
  - ❌ The concert was sold out; therefore I watched a movie at home.
  - ✅ The concert was sold out, so I watched a movie at home.
- **hence**: formal. Its clause-linking use ("; hence we conclude...") is rare outside scientific and mathematical writing. In general prose, prefer the noun-phrase construction ("hence the delay") or use "therefore" or "so".

## How to apply this when editing

1. Scan for each occurrence of "thus", "therefore" and "hence".
2. If a comma precedes the word and an independent clause follows, flag it as a comma splice and propose the fix that best fits the text's register: a period or semicolon in formal writing, "so" in informal writing.
3. If a noun phrase follows "hence" or an -ing phrase follows "thus", leave the comma alone.
4. Check register: in a casual email or chat message, a heavy "therefore" or "hence" may read as stiff; suggest "so". In a paper or report, "so" at the start of a sentence may read as too casual; suggest "thus" or "therefore".
5. Check meaning: if "therefore" links two facts without a real logical inference, suggest "so" instead.
6. When explaining a change, keep it short, for example: "'Therefore' is an adverb and can't join two clauses with just a comma, so I used a semicolon."

Don't flag missing commas directly after the adverbs as errors. They are a style choice.
